# Profit, Loss and Mixture — the brief for each topic

Five topics, kept as their own subject because they were asked for
separately and because they are arithmetic rather than advanced maths: they
are asked in every tier, including the ones with no advanced-maths section.

**The thing to be exact about.** Every percentage here is a percentage *of*
something, and the whole chapter's difficulty is which something. Profit per
cent is on the **cost price** unless the question says otherwise; discount
per cent is on the **marked price**; the gain of a shopkeeper using a false
weight is on the **weight he actually gives**. Every formula you write must
say what it is a percentage of, and each topic needs a careful box on its own
version of this confusion.

Money is written `₹1,200`. Use clean numbers: the answer should come out whole
or to one decimal place.

---

## profit-basics — "Cost price, selling price and the percentage"

- `Profit = SP − CP`, `Loss = CP − SP`, and
  **`Profit% = (Profit/CP) × 100`** — on the cost price.
- **`SP = CP(100 + p)/100`** and **`CP = SP × 100/(100 + p)`**; the loss
  versions with `(100 − l)`. Going backwards from SP to CP by subtracting the
  percentage is the standard mistake and deserves the careful box.
- Profit reckoned on the selling price instead: if the profit is `x%` of the
  SP, then on the CP it is **`100x/(100 − x)`%**, and the other way round
  `100x/(100 + x)`.
- **Two articles sold at the same price, one at a gain of `x%` and the other
  at a loss of `x%`: the result is always a loss of `x²/100` per cent**, and
  never a break-even. One of the most asked results in the chapter.
- `n` articles sold at the price of `m`: `profit% = ((m − n)/n) × 100`.
- The CP of `n` articles equals the SP of `m` articles:
  `profit% = ((n − m)/m) × 100`.
- Goods bought at "`x` for ₹`y`" and sold at "`p` for ₹`q`".
- If the SP is doubled, what happens to the profit per cent; the SP needed
  for a target profit per cent; the SP that would turn a given loss into a
  given gain.
- A trader selling part of his stock at a gain and the rest at a loss, and the
  overall result.
- Profit per cent when the CP and SP are in a given ratio.

## profit-discount — "Marked price, discount and successive discounts"

- `Discount = MP − SP`, **`Discount% = (Discount/MP) × 100`** — on the marked
  price — and **`SP = MP(100 − d)/100`**.
- **Two successive discounts of `a%` and `b%` are a single discount of
  `(a + b − ab/100)%`** — never `a + b`. Three successive discounts by
  applying it twice.
- Marked `x%` above cost and then sold at `d%` discount:
  **`profit% = [(100 + x)(100 − d)/100] − 100`**. The two standard cases:
  marked 40% up with 10% off gives 26% profit; marked 20% up with 20% off
  gives a 4% loss, not break-even.
- The marked price needed to allow a given discount and still make a given
  profit.
- Which is better for the buyer: a single discount or a given pair of
  successive ones.
- "Buy 2 get 1 free" is a discount of `33⅓%`; "buy 3 get 1 free" is `25%`;
  a scheme giving `x` free with `y` bought.
- Two shops offering different discount structures on the same marked price.
- The relation `CP : MP : SP` as a ratio, and recovering the percentages
  from it.
- A discount given on the list price for cash, on top of a trade discount.

## profit-dishonest — "False weights and the dishonest shopkeeper"

- A shopkeeper who sells at cost price but gives less than he charges for:
  **`gain% = (error/(true value − error)) × 100`** — the denominator is what
  the customer actually receives, and using the true weight instead is the
  single commonest error in the chapter. Careful box, with the numbers:
  900 g sold as 1 kg gives `100/9 = 11 1/9 %`, not 10%.
- The same formula read backwards: given the gain per cent, find the weight
  used.
- A false weight **and** a markup together: multiply the two factors —
  `(true/given) × (100 + x)/100`.
- A faulty measure of length or volume — a metre stick of 96 cm, a litre
  measure of 950 ml.
- A dealer who is cheated when buying and cheats when selling: both errors
  compound.
- Mixing water with milk and selling at the cost price of milk: the gain is
  the water's share, `(water/milk) × 100`.
- Selling at a loss on paper while gaining by weight, and whether the net
  result is a gain.
- Reducing the price by `x%` so that a customer gets `y` more for the same
  money — the `(100x)/(100 − x)` relation between a price fall and the extra
  quantity.

## mix-allig — "Mixture and alligation"

- The **rule of alligation**:
  **`(quantity of cheaper)/(quantity of dearer) = (dearer price − mean
  price)/(mean price − cheaper price)`.** Give the cross diagram in words,
  because that is how it is used.
- The mean price of a mixture made in a given ratio, and the ratio needed for
  a given mean price.
- Which side of the alligation goes on top — the standard slip is inverting
  the ratio, and the check is that more of the cheaper means a mean nearer
  the cheaper.
- Milk and water: water costs nothing, so adding water to milk is alligation
  with a zero price; the gain per cent from selling the mixture at the price
  of pure milk.
- How much water to add to a given mixture to bring the ratio to `x : y`;
  how much of one variety to add to change a mixture's mean price.
- Two vessels holding different ratios, mixed in equal measures and in a
  given ratio — the standard two-vessel question.
- **Alligation outside mixtures**, which is where most of its marks are: two
  rates of interest averaging a given return, two speeds averaging a given
  speed, two batches of goods averaging a given profit per cent, a class's
  average mark from two groups. Three questions at least, because the
  technique is the same and students rarely see it.
- Alligation with three ingredients, taken in pairs.

## mix-replace — "Replacement, ratios and three-vessel mixing"

- **The replacement formula**: if `x` litres are drawn from `v` litres of pure
  liquid and replaced with water, `n` times over, the liquid left is
  **`v(1 − x/v)ⁿ`**, so the fraction remaining is `(1 − x/v)ⁿ`.
- The same formula when the vessel already holds a mixture, applied to the
  component being removed.
- Given the final amount, find `n`; given `n` and the final ratio, find `x`.
- A vessel with milk and water in the ratio `a : b`: how much mixture must be
  drawn off and replaced with water to make the ratio `c : d`.
- Removing part of a mixture and adding one component only — the component
  removed goes down in the mixture's own ratio, which is the step most often
  skipped.
- Three vessels of the same capacity holding different ratios, poured
  together: add the parts after putting all three ratios over a common
  denominator.
- The ratio after adding both components in a given quantity.
- A mixture sold and replaced, where the question asks for the **original**
  quantity: work backwards from the final ratio.
- Two mixtures combined so that the final ratio is given — alligation applied
  to the fraction of one component, which is the elegant route and worth
  showing beside the long one.
